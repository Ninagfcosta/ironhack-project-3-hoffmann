# EU AI Act Approval Pack

**Prepared by:** [Janaina Hoffmann]
**Date:** [13.08.2026]

## Quick Summary

I looked at 4 AI ideas from clients. One of them is just not allowed at all. One is risky and needs a lot of extra rules before it can be used. One just needs to tell people "hey, I'm a robot." And one is totally fine as is.

---

## Case 1 — Store Cameras Guessing Feelings + Surprise Discounts

**Category:** Prohibited (not allowed)

**How it would work:**
- Trigger: a shopper walks into camera range
- What the AI does: looks at the shopper's face/body to guess how they feel
- Does a human check it first? No
- What happens next: a discount pops up on a screen automatically
- Who builds it: the AI company selling this camera tool
- Who uses it: the store chain

**Why it's a problem:** The store is spying on people's feelings without asking, just to trick them into buying more. That's exactly the kind of sneaky move the law says you can't do.

**My decision:** **Say no to this idea.**

**A better idea instead:** Instead of secretly reading emotions, let customers sign up for a loyalty card on purpose. Then give them discounts based on what they've bought before (not their feelings). Tell them clearly how it works, and let them opt out anytime.

## Case 2 — AI Sorting Resumes for a Job Agency

**Category:** High-risk

**How it would work:**
- Trigger: a recruiter opens a new job posting
- What the AI does: reads resumes and puts them in order, best to worst
- Does a human check it first? Only the top 10 get checked by a person
- What happens next: top resumes go to interview, the rest get auto-rejected
- Who builds it: the company selling the resume- sorting tool
- Who uses it: the job agency

**Why it needs extra care:** This AI is deciding who even gets a shot at a job that's a big deal for people's lives, so the law wants humans watching closely, tests to check it's not biased, and records kept of everything.

**My decision:** **Okay to use, but only with some fixes**  a real person needs to also check the resumes that got auto- rejected (not just the top 10), the company needs to test the AI for bias, and they need to tell candidates an AI was involved.

## Case 3 — Chat Robot Answering Store Questions

**Category:** Limited risk / needs to be upfront about being a robot

**How it would work:**
- Trigger: a customer opens the chat box
- What the AI does: answers easy questions about orders and returns
- Does a human check it first? Only jumps in if the question is too hard
- What happens next: the customer gets an answer, or gets passed to a human
- Who builds it: the chatbot company
- Who uses it: the online store

**Why it needs a small rule:** It's not making any risky decision about someone's life, so it's mostly fine — the only thing the law asks is that the robot tells people "hey, I'm an AI, not a person."

**My decision:** **Okay to use, just add a message** like "You're chatting with an AI assistant" at the start, and always let people reach a real human if they want.

## Case 4 — AI Suggesting How Much to Bake

**Category:** Minimal risk

**How it would work:**
- Trigger: the week's sales numbers come in
- What the AI does: suggests how much bread/cake to make tomorrow
- Does a human check it first? Yes, the manager can change it anytime
- What happens next: bakery orders ingredients based on the (maybe adjusted) suggestion
- Who builds it: a small AI tool or in-house system
- Who uses it: the bakery

**Why it's no big deal:** It's just a helpful tip, and the manager stays in control the whole time. It doesn't hurt anyone or make important decisions about people, so there's no special rule to follow.

**My decision:** **Totally fine, go ahead.**
